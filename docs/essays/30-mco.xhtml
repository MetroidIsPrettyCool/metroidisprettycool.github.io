You’re Probably Wrong About Mars Climate Orbiter
It’s Not About Inches Vs. Centimeters

(This one is more of a response.)

See, a while ago I came across this talk, “Programming’s Greatest Mistakes”, from one Mark Rendle:

I have no problem with its overall message (manage your projects well, stay organized, make sure you know what you’re doing, etc.) but I was a little irked seeing him bring up Mars Climate Orbiter. I’m sick and tired of seeing it misrepresented again and again by supposedly reputable sources as just a mere units mix-up. Y’know, “oh those stupid Americans and their inches and feet, don’t they know that’ll get them in trouble when they try doing real science?” and so forth.

That’s not what happened.

I mean, first off: it wasn’t a mix-up of inches and centimeters, it was a mix-up of pound-force-seconds and newton-seconds; and it didn’t crash into Mars, it was simply too low when they went in for an aerobrake pass (we don’t know where it ended up); and it definitely didn’t leave “a scar that you can see from the other orbiters”. Mark is probably either thinking of Beagle 2 or Mars Polar Lander, or perhaps one of the more recent unmanned lunar landers. (Beresheet?)

But, more to the point: the MCO phase I and phase II mishap reports are just PDFs you can find online. You can read them – they’re really not that unapproachable – and find the takeaways that NASA Safety and Mission Assurance thought everyone else needed to learn. You don’t have to just guess what the problem was from internet rumors, like Mark did!

The important bits for us right now are these ones:

Root Cause:

Failure to use metric units in the coding of a ground software file, “Small Forces,” used in trajectory models

Contributing Causes:

  1. Undetected mismodeling of spacecraft velocity changes
  2. Navigation Team unfamiliar with spacecraft
  3. Trajectory correction maneuver number 5 not performed
  4. System engineering process did not adequately address transition from development to operations
  5. Inadequate communications between project elements
  6. Inadequate operations Navigation Team staffing
  7. Inadequate training
  8. Verification and validation process did not adequately address ground software

(Stephenson et al., 1999, p. 7)

The Mars Climate Orbiter mission was conducted under NASA’s “Faster, Better, Cheaper” philosophy, developed in recent years to enhance innovation, productivity and cost-effectiveness of America’s space program. […] The success of “Faster, Better, Cheaper” is tempered by the fact that some projects and programs have put too much emphasis on cost and schedule reduction (the “Faster” and “Cheaper” elements of the paradigm). At the same time, they have failed to instill sufficient rigor in risk management throughout the mission lifecycle. These actions have increased risk to an unacceptable level on these projects.

The Mishap Investigation Board conducted a series of meetings over several months with the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and Lockheed Martin Astronautics to better understand the issues that led to the failure of the Mars Climate Orbiter. […] More importantly, the project failed to introduce sufficient discipline in the processes used to develop, validate and operate the spacecraft; nor did it adequately instill a mission success culture that would shore up the risk introduced by these cuts. These process and project leadership deficiencies introduced sufficient risk to compromise mission success to the point of mission failure.

…

…the Board found that a significant infrastructure of processes and requirements already is in place to enable robust program and project management. However, these processes are not being adequately implemented within the context of “Faster, Better, Cheaper.” To move toward the ideal vision of Mission Success First, the Board makes a series of observations and recommendations that are grouped into four categories, providing a guide by which to measure progress.

(Stephenson et al., 2000, p. 6)

Does that sound like they just needed to standardize on metric units? Let’s be serious here. Professionals do not make mistakes this big for no reason. NASA are not complete idiots. They already know how to run million dollar programs and get it right, if they have the time, margins and money to do it properly. The MCO team did not have any of these. They took the gamble anyway, and they lost.

In fact, they lost pretty abysmally. Something like a third of the MCO Phase I report is recommendations for how to avoid something similar on the contemporaneous Mars Polar Lander (MPL) mission, and then that failed too, shortly before touchdown, just less than a month later; and then both of the ride-along Deep Space 2 impactor probes failed before they could carry out their mission; and Mars Surveyor ’98 was 0 for 4 and out of spacecraft. Some three hundred and fifty million dollars down the drain. That’s when the technical reports really started coming in. (e.g. Young et al., 2000)

Why? Because this wasn’t an isolated incident. These were the 90s at NASA! Dan Goldin was administrator! “Faster, Better, Cheaper” (pick three) was the paradigm for the whole space program!

Of the ten missions launched between 1998 and 19991, five of them conclusively failed (MCO, DS2, MPL, WIRE, TERRIERS), one of them partially failed before being recovered to “partial success” with great effort (DS1), and four were successes (Lunar Prospector, Stardust, SWAS and TRACE).

Expand our view to Goldin’s whole administratorship, and we find several more examples: the failures of Lewis and CONTOUR, the near-failure of the Genesis sample return, the failures of Clementine and NEAR Shoemaker during their extended missions, the constant issues with the Wake Shield Facility, the STS-51 Transfer Orbit Stage cradle explosion, the near-disaster of STS-93, the worrying normalization of deviance on STS-95 – I could go on and on.

Actually, I will go on, because this is a problem bigger than just Dan Goldin – easy to make fun of though he is. Similar numbers of accidents surround the NASA throughout the 80s, 90s and 2000s, regardless of administrator.

The problem was then, and is now, Congress.

Reducing costs to make risks more worthwhile is not ipso facto a bad idea, if you still have the time, budget and margins to get it done. Likewise: a broader scope, conservative planning and a bigger budget is also not a bad idea, if you still have the time, budget and margins to get it done.

NASA has struggled so much with project management since the 60s because, honestly, it can’t do effective project management. There are intractable upfront costs there that cannot be reconciled with what the legislature demands. Every year another report comes out with the same conclusions and the same recommendations and every year those recommendations slip or get dropped or ignored or forgotten, because there’s no safety regulation you can pass that lets you do a $20 job for $15.

That’s the real lesson. Put up for your team, or they’re going to have do what you’re asking for. Not what you want, not what works, not what you need.

Let’s hope you’re not putting lives on the line.
