1. Programming

1.1. Editors

1.1.1. Editor War?

Emacs forever! (cl-loop while (rules :emacs) while (drools :vi) do (message "you already know it"))

Vi – and its issue – are text editors. They do many things, and they do not do all of them well. They open files, they insert text into files, they change text in files, they launch subcommands, they run shells, they multiplex, they do IPC, they gesture at extensibility. All in just one program. (Not what you were expecting me to say?)

Emacs is an environment. It is a guest VM with host filesystem access (and remote filesystem access) and ten thousand preinstalled utilities for text manipulation. search-forward-regexp, magit-status, default-indent-new-line, eshell, eww – these are not commands. They’re single purpose, well-made, filtering, plain text-acting, inter-operating programs. You can even go look up their source code, whenever you want! M-x find-function RET foo-bar RET.1

Who’s the real UNIX philosophy exemplar here? ed with bolt-ons? Or the purpose-built, hackable, extensible, cohesive software distribution?

You can even use Elisp programs in your shell scripts! (With pipes!) Some trivial examples:

I admit it’s less flexible when you yank it out into shell-land like this, but why should it be flexible? The ecosystem is the point of the ecosystem. The interactivity is the point of the interactivity.

If I find I have a real need to move some data around between Emacs and a shell program, I’ve got so many more options from within than I do without:

  1. There’s the inferior shell, invoked with the command M-x shell;
  2. and the Eshell, invoked with the command M-x eshell;
  3. and inferior terminal mode, AKA term-mode, invoked with the commands M-x term or M-x ansi-term (depending on what exactly you want, behavior-wise).
  4. Then there’s the command shell-command, invoked with M-! or M-x shell-command, or with a prefix argument (C-u M-!, C-u M-x shell-command) to insert output into the current buffer, or called directly from lisp via M-: (shell-command COMMAND &optional OUTPUT-BUFFER ERROR-BUFFER) RET;
  5. and the command shell-command-on-region, invoked with M-| or M-x shell-command-on-region, or with a prefix argument (C-u M-|, C-u M-x shell-command-on-region) to replace the given region with the results.
  6. There’s the command dired-do-shell-command, bound by default to ! in dired-mode and also invocable with M-x dired-do-shell-command.
  7. There’s the lisp function (make-process), invocable interactively with M-: (make-process &rest ARGS);
  8. and the lisp function (call-process), invocable interactively with M-: (call-process PROGRAM &optional INFILE DESTINATION DISPLAY &rest ARGS);
  9. and the lisp function (call-process-region), invocable interactively with M-: (call-process-region START END PROGRAM &optional DELETE BUFFER DISPLAY &rest ARGS).

And guess what? Commands are merely a specially-marked kind of lisp function, so they’re also usable anywhere a non-command lisp function would be, such as being invoked explicitly via M-:, from within Elisp scripts, and from within other functions. All are customizable and hackable and extensible and re-usable and documented.

Everything in Emacs is documented! Want the help page for the function foobar? C-h f foobar, and as a bonus you get whatever key bindings will invoke it (if any). Want the help page for the variable foobar? C-h v foobar. Want both? (Elisp is a Lisp-2.) C-h o foobar. Want to search for something in the Emacs info manual? C-h r for info-emacs-manual, then s in the info window for Info-search, then just type what you’re searching for. Repeat the search until you find it. What about the Elisp info manual? C-h R elisp for info-display-manual, then s and the thing you’re searching for like before. Want a specific info top-level manual, like, for example, the eshell manual? C-h R eshell. Not sure what info manuals are installed? C-h i. Need a man page instead? M-x man. Not sure what keybindings are available in a given major-mode? C-h m lists the major mode of a window, and all the minor modes, and all their key bindings. Want to know what function a key binding runs? C-h k, and then type the key binding, e.g. C-h k C-x C-f opens the help page for find-file. It even works for mouse bindings, too!

I could go on and on. What do Bash and Vi give me, in exchange for surrendering notions of buffers, regions, points, kill rings, kmacros, in-editor documentation, so on and so forth? Worse scripting languages? “Job control”?

No thanks.

1.1.2. Tabs vs. Spaces?

Doesn’t matter. Manual indentation is for monkeys on typewriters. (Significant whitespace is for idiots who wish they were monkeys on typewriters.) As long as you, the maintainer, are willing to put in five minutes to enforce your norms with an .editorconfig,2 I’ll use whatever you want. Otherwise, M-x tabify, M-x untabify, and M-x indent-according-to-mode are my friends.

1.2. Paradigms

1.2.1. Functional vs. OOP?

  1. Something something “true OOP has never been tried” something something “actually, I think you’ll find it works really well in Smalltalk/Objective C before it got bad/my custom fork of the Common Lisp interpreter/some other language that, conspicuously, nobody is using to ship real software.”

    Perhaps, among all the many languages which call themselves “object-oriented”, there exists one example which makes writing good software easy and bad software hard. Myself? I have not found such a language. The state of those following Java’s lead is particularly dire…3

    Why must we only encapsulate on object boundaries? Why must we eschew structuring programs as “collections of things to do” for “collections of things we can do stuff to”? Does that not read as inside-out to people?

  2. “Pure functions” are a very elegant idea, on this I agree. I am less sold on the universality of first-order functions. Perhaps it only really shines when you get strict about your principles? I admit, most of my experience with FP has been in multiparadigm languages. Lisp, Rust, etc.

What I really want out of a language is first-class recognition that subprograms are heterogeneous. Some are pure, others stateful; some are sync, some are async; some run on servers, some run on clients; some parts on run CPUs, others on GPUs, or NPUs, or DSPs; some require SIMD, others MIMD; some run natively, others in browsers, others sandboxed, or in bytecode VMs; they interact with hierarchical caches; they interact with branch prediction; they talk to each other, they talk to themselves; and so on, and so on.

I would like a language which recognizes this, and makes it easy to tell the compiler what behavior it should expect of a variable, or a subprogram, or system resource. Not simply the things that it does, but equally importantly the things it doesn’t do. We might then modularize our software on boundaries of shared effects and capabilities, each sub-module further constraining the possibility space until it is trivial to reason about.

The more we can reason, the more powerful the metaprogramming we can provide. Each bottom-level module can settle into its own pseudo-DSL, describing behavior as befits its nature. “I am a collection of pure arithmetic functions on floats, here are some equations, please convert them to optimal SIMD”, “I am a description of a protocol, here is my specification, prove its soundness and provide implementations of the things it describes”, “I am a set of tasks, here is they require, what they do, and what they provide, please construct the backing parallel runtime and data structures to run them in”, “I am a UI, here are my elements and their relationships, please construct an event loop to run them and the means to update my state” and so on and so forth.

Wouldn’t that be wonderful? To be able to imply parallelization, or IPC, or fronted-backend, or coprocessor offload, all with just type signatures? At least for trivial cases?

I understand that there are optimizations compilers will never intuit. A lot of optimizations they’ll never intuit. I hope that, for those cases, this approach would still make it easier to write programs than it would make them harder.

I suspect the answer lies somewhere between LISP, Ada, CUDA, Julia, perhaps OpenMP, functional programming; but I confess I’m too uninformed to say anything definitive. Call me back when I’m more experienced?

1.2.2. Dynamic vs. Static Typing?

“Check it out guys: we managed to save a whole year building this new airliner by never checking if we bought the right materials!”

I can accept dynamic typing for projects with less than a hundred SLOC. Two hundred is a perhaps. Three hundred is really pushing it. Four hundred is way too big to reason about.

If your language has types, I don’t understand why you would want to wait as long as possible to tell me I’ve used them wrong. You aren’t saving me from errors, the error was there to begin with!

1.2.3. Strong vs. Weak Typing?

“Check it out guys: we managed to save a whole year building this new airliner by re-using all these old washing machine bolts! The thread pitches are different, but we just cranked ’em down anyways! They’re almost flush, even!”

Weak typing does not reduce program complexity, it just makes it harder to understand the complexity that was already there. I do not want to add a integer to a string. That is not a well-formed operation. I cannot predict its behavior. I can guess, but I’ll never know for sure, unless I read the standard.

More to the point, if I can read the standard and therein I see that String + Integer actually means “convert this integer to a string with .toString() and then .join() them”, then what benefit does this ambiguous + operator actually give me over an explicit String.join(Integer.toString())? Golf cred? Screw golf cred. I want to write what I mean.

1.3. Hardware

1.3.1. AMD vs. Intel?

Intel are an amusing company. Their in-house architectures all suck, their CPUs implementing those architectures suck, their CPUs implementing other people’s architectures suck, their attempts to innovate always crash and burn, and somehow they’re still number 1. How does that even happen?

I’d like to see one of their wacky ideas finally pay off one day. ’Til then? Yeah, you should probably just buy AMD.

1.3.2. RISC vs. CISC?

Nobody is really making new “the ISA is the metal” processors these days, it’s all compromised. Did you know that ARM has a instruction just for JavaScript? FJCVTZS, AKA “Floating-point Javascript convert to signed fixed-point, rounding toward zero”.4

I think I’ll just abstain on this one, and say go with whatever happens to have the best numbers at the moment.

1.4. Ideology

1.4.1. LLMs and “Vibe Coding”?

The most important fact to internalize about large language models is that they’re profoundly stupid. They will lie, they will overstate, they will misunderstand, and they will be biased. Never make a decision regarding LLMs that does not centralize this fact.

The second most important fact to internalize is that they cannot tell you if they are confident in their answers, because they have no capacity for self-reflection. When’s the last time you asked ChatGPT a question and replied asking for your help? Has it ever happened? Never make a decision on the basis of an LLM’s input without considering this too.

Evangelists may attempt to convince you that the solution to these problems is only a matter of time. It is not. It is an inherent consequence of the “bigger autocomplete” approach.

It is still possible to get real, productive value out of LLMs – particularly if you don’t part with your money while doing so – but only when these facts do not present problems. There are circumstances where wrong answers are more useful than no answers. There are also circumstances where their answers are trivial to prove or disprove. These are the path. Stick tight, Little Red Riding Hood, and you won’t have any regrets.

I cannot say the same for faith in miracles.

1.4.2. UNIX philosophy?

Despite what I might have implied earlier, I don’t really care about the UNIX philosophy.

It’s a decent set of principles for writing coreutils, but quickly grow inane when you try to apply them to anything real. What even is “a program”? I’ve never seen a good answer from the diehards that doesn’t contradict what they seem to believe in practice.

Look closer, and you’ll find that most applications are, in fact, collections of smaller programs that all do one thing well.
