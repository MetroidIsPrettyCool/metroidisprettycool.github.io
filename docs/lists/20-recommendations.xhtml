1.2. Meanwhile (2010) by Jason Shiga

This one is more of a recommendation for you to give. Meanwhile is aimed pretty solidly at a late elementary, middle school demographic – that’s not to say you can’t read it as an adult, of course, but it’s short and can only get so complex.

Meanwhile is… hmmm. How to describe it. Meanwhile was and still is very influential on how I read and understand Art.

It’s a choose-your-own-adventure graphic novel about choice, framed by the (fictionalized) many worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. Panel flow is dictated by following these little noodle-y flowchart lines that connect panels, rather than typical page layout rules, and decisions for the reader are represented by forks in these paths. When you’re asked if you want chocolate or vanilla ice cream, for example, you follow the line that connects to the panel of your choice. (The whole thing is printed on laminated paper, which can help younger children physically trace the path with their finger.) Everything unfolds from here in a sort of “House of Leaves for ten-year-olds” kind of way.

Diegetically, readers make use of a time machine, a mind reading machine (which can read the minds of dead people), and an “instantaneously kill everyone in the entire world except (optionally) yourself” machine to slowly unravel the mystery at the heart of the book; while at the same time, Shiga is deliberately interweaving panels from separate timelines together onto the same page, so that nondiegetically you the reader are also constantly getting these little contextless flashes of what could be, what might be, what was, what wasn’t – everything happening in the meanwhile.

You have to read and re-read it over and over again, coming at it from different angles, “cheating” by just flipping through cover-to-cover, re-reading as a complete rules stickler, and really just thoroughly exercise it to get full understanding.

It’s such a unique, beautiful little thing. If you don’t want to take my word for it, why not Scott McCloud’s? (Author of Understanding Comics, among others.)

This book is going to be so, so, cool!

Many of us cartoonists already own Jason Shiga‘s original hand-printed version of this insane choose-own-adventure masterpiece (he even has a quote from me), but the idea of a full-out professionally printed version with tabs. Ah, be still my heart…

Best of all is the absolute certainty that whenever we see the number “3,856” used to describe the many branching possibilities, it was Shiga himself that came up with the number and the number’s gonna be correct.

—Scott McCloud, February 17th, 2010 (https://www.scottmccloud.com/2010/02/17/3856-story-possibilities/)

Currently, it’s available in digital form and physical hardcover. Also possibly from your local library. Check it out!

1.3. Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind (1982) by Hayao Miyazaki

The manga, not the movie. Though the movie is interesting in its own way, its a very different thing.

See, the manga entered serialization in 1982, and the movie came out in 1984, but then the manga kept going until 1994. Those ten years of distance gave Miyazaki a lot of time to rethink his message, and as a result the manga ending is a pretty direct refutation of the film ending that’s less… pleasant, but more compelling.

If you liked the darker direction of Princess Mononoke (which, notably, started production immediately after Nausicaä ended) but thought it still didn’t have quite enough of a biting edge, I really recommend you read Nausicaä.

In short, you are a god. One of the many gods created a millennium ago.

And over that millennium you have become covered with sarcoma and filth.

I do not doubt that you were created out of idealism and a sense of purpose in an age of despair.

Why didn’t those men and women realize that both purity and corruption are the very stuff of life?

Suffering and tragedy and folly will not disappear in a purified world. They are part of humanity. That is why, even in a world of suffering, there can also be joy and shining light.

[…] Because you were created as an artifical god of purity, you have become the ugliest creature of all, never knowing what it means to be alive.

--Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind, Volume II, pg. 510

If you are light, then we do not need light.

--Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind, Volume II, pg. 518

There’s a few different versions floating around bookstores these days. A decent summary of all the differences can be found here: http://www.nausicaa.net/wiki/Nausica%C3%A4_(Manga_Availability)

The easiest release to obtain is probably going to be the “deluxe” two-volume hardcover box set, which is in most respects the most definitive version, save for some typos in the English translation. (A summary of them can be found here https://nyaa.si/view/1213298) If you can’t or don’t want to go with the deluxe set, then I suggest you at least try to avoid the “flipped” RTL versions. Why oh why did localizers ever think that was a good idea?

2. Movies / Television

2.1. 28 Years Later (2025) dir. Danny Boyle

Ouuuggghhh. The tone in this film. Apparently the reviews were pretty split between critics and audiences? I have no idea how you could dislike 28 Years Later. It’s so bold, so visually inventive, so overwhelmingly bittersweet. In an overwhelming world, that means a lot.

If you liked 28 Days Later, then – actually, it doesn’t matter if you’ve seen or care about 28 Days. This is a spiritual sequel, not really a plot sequel.

If you like films with strong themes and presentation to match, you gotta watch 28 Years Later. You gotta.

2.2. The Animatrix (2003) dir. Various

Sort of a mixed bag, IMO, but which contents of the bag are which seems up to popular debate.

I won’t push you in any particular direction, then, but I will shout out my favorite short of the bunch: Beyond, written and directed by Koji Morimoto. It’s got a really unique perspective on Life in Computer World, and I love its sense of wonder. Nothing comes close.

You might also know Morimoto from his work on Magnetic Rose from the Memories anthology, or Franken’s Gears from the Robot Carnival anthology. He’s real big on short films.

2.3. The Black Hole (1979) dir. Gary Nelson

Surprising good, given how bad it is. Or maybe it’s surprising bad, given how good it is.

The set (incl. miniatures + matte painting) design is as striking as it is utterly unique, except for all the boring gray-brown metal corridors they spend half the runtime in. The performances are flat and boring 70s Disney nothing, except for Maximillian Schell, who spends the whole film behaving as though he’s forgotten what a movie is and every scene is his first introduction to the concept – totally electrifying. The robots characters range from “Slim Pickins as cuddly teddy bear trashcan” to “mute, hovering Swiss Army Knife possessed by Satan himself (more or less)”. The ending is one of the most bizarre things I’ve ever seen in any film, ever.

You may not like it as a film, but there’s a lot of stuff to pick through here that you won’t find anywhere else.

2.4. Cinderella III: A Twist in Time (2007) dir. Frank Nissen

Frank Nissen set out to make the best film he could, circumstances be damned, and by god he succeeded.

You should watch it. I swear it’s not nearly as contrived or boring as you might expect. (Though it does suffer from some of the jank that’s characteristic of direct-to-dvd sequels. Just not enough to really knock it down.)

2.5. Crank 2: High Voltage (2009) dir. Mark Neveldine and Brian Taylor

The Freddy Got Fingered (2001) of action films. It’s sort of hard to get across how much bullshit happens without just listing the plot summary. Here’s one, though: wanna see a suitmation Kaiju fight with Jason Statham wearing a giant Spitting Image-esque helmet of his own face? If yes, and you can live with sitting through one of the most racist, sexist, homophobic movies to ever see a theatrical release, then I highly recommend you check this one out.

2.8. Sunshine (2007) dir. Danny Boyle

The other movie where Cillian Murphy plays a tortured physicist that builds a giant bomb. That guy was made in a lab to play characters that look like they always kind of want to kill themselves, I swear.

Everything you’d expect from Boyle and Garland working together, further bolstered by a retrospectively bananas cast list. Chris Evans, Cillian Murphy, Michelle Yeoh, Hiroyuki Sanada, and Cliff Curtis? For 40 million dollars? Unbelievable.

Sunshine looks great, sounds great, and no matter what Tarantino might tell you, carries a strong narrative throughline all the way to the end. Like 28 Years Later, this is a film that no one else could have ever made. At minimum I think you should see it just for the completely original space suit design. That, or the John Murphy/Underworld soundtrack – take your pick.

3. Music

Music is both easier and harder to recommend, because there’s so much of it but it’s so easy to casually listen to. Gonna try to dig a lot deeper here to avoid the obvious picks, you already know the stuff that’s good and popular.

3.1. Asuka Re(build) by Free Parking!

This is the album that really made me fall in love with Free Parking!, the title tracks “Asuka” and “Asuka, continued” in particular. Brendan Steere – whom you might know as the writer and director of the The Velocipastor, this is his high school band – knows what he’s talking about.

3.2. Humanistic (2001) by Abandoned Pools

Tommy Walter is criminally underrated. Criminally!

If you know him from anywhere, it’s probably from his stint as the bass player for Eels’ first album (more complicated than that, I digress), or the work he did for MTV’s animated TV show Clone High. Most of the latter comes from this album, a great little indie electro-pop/alternative rock record. Special shout-out to “Start Over”, my pick for the best song off the album.

If you find you like Humanistic, the rest of Walter’s discography is also worth a listen. He’s got a few other one-off projects, plus fourish-fivish other albums as Abandoned Pools; The Reverb EP, Armed to the Teeth, Somnambulist (all as Abandoned Pools) and The Color by Number EP (as Glacier Hiking, with Ross Golan) are more rock-focused, and Sublime Currency, The Haunted House (both as Abandoned Pools), Button Pusher (as Oliver the Penguin, with Rie Sinclair) lean poppier. Supraliminal (as Tommy Walter) is kind of doing its own thing. Feels like an orchestral horror film score, all instrumental.

3.4. IN MY HEAD by vivivivivi

“Credits Song for My Death” really blew up a while back – I have some thoughts on that which I’ll keep to myself – but I think a lot of that failed to trickle back to the rest of the album, or indeed the artist herself.

3.9. The Very Best of Stan Rogers by Stan Rogers

Recommending a greatest hits album when talking music is kind of a cop-out move, but the poor guy died so young (in a fire aboard a grounded aircraft no less, really unspeakably tragic) that this really does represent most of his studio recorded songs. It’s a goddamned shame.

Anyways, if you like folk or sea shanties, it’s hard to get much better than Stan Rogers. The guy was Canadian Maritime through and through. My personal favorites off this record are “Barrett’s Privateers”, “Forty-Five Years”, “Lock-Keeper” and “The Mary Ellen Carter”. (Also, if you’re a programmer or technically inclined like I am, you should give “The White Collar Holler” from Between the Breaks … Live! a listen – it’s very funny. “And it’s Ho, boys, can’t you code it, and program it right / Nothing ever happens in the life of mine / I’m hauling up the data on the Xerox line”)

4. Video Games

4.1. Crystalis (1990)

The NES one, not that cut-down Game Boy Color port.

Crystalis wears its influences pretty blatantly, but this a great thing, because Crystalis is like if Zelda 1 was good. Most of the same mechanics, but deeper, with better graphics, better music, real writing (not a lot, but it’s impressive for the NES!), and more content. You also use magic to disguise yourself as a girl in order to infiltrate a standoffish all-female society a full eight years before Ocarina of Time did the same thing.

You can play it right now on Nintendo Switch Online, or via the SNK 40th Anniversary Collection. Or by getting a cartridge off eBay. Or… uh, other means.

4.2. Fallout: New Vegas (2010)

Real in a way that Bethesda could never comprehend. C’mon Todd, is it really so hard to give us some verisimilar faction psychologies?

I don’t remember where I first heard it, so apologies for the lack of credit, but the best way I’ve seen it put is this: In Fallout 4, there’s a guy who runs a baseball stand. He dresses like a 50s baseball player, talks like a 50s baseball player, makes up outlandish claims about the sport, and tries to sell you related paraphernalia. He is a one-note joke about how everyone in the future will be stupid because they don’t know what baseball is. In Fallout: New Vegas, the Legion all wear football pads because it’s the most plentiful source of light body armor they have. None of ’em give a single shit about football.

Or, more bluntly: Obsidian’s Fallout is about the complicated relationship people have with the past – old world or otherwise – and Bethesda’s Fallout is about how goofy the 1950s were, with some tech-themed sword and sorcery slop thrown in for a main quest.

Maybe don’t play the game vanilla, though. The Viva New Vegas modding guide is probably the canonical reference here, so make sure to check that out: https://vivanewvegas.moddinglinked.com/. Shouldn’t be too painful on Windows. On Linux, well, all I can really say is that it does work eventually. Eventually…

4.4. Marathon (1994), Marathon 2: Durandal (1995), Marathon Infinity (1996)

So it turns out, John,2 that video games can be good first person shooters and have compelling writing at the same time. Duh!

No we’re not talking about modern Bungie’s ill-conceived extraction shooter “reboot”, we’re talking about the 1994 OG. The first FPS with mouselook. The co-first FPS with dual-wielding. Half-Life before Half-Life. The criminally underappreciated also-ran for the Macintosh. The original Bungie philosophical Sci-Fi action title about a lone cyborg and his AI friend facing off against a coalition of evil aliens trying to destroy a human colony.

Marathon looks like Doom, but plays a bit more like Portal (the first one) or a level-based Metroid Prime. Lots of terminals to read, lore with great thematic cohesion, compelling characters, puzzles, lots of fun bugs to shoot. Even better, all three games are free on Steam!

A suggestion for when you first play Marathon 1: set “Rendering System” to “OpenGL” and turn on “3D perspective” (Graphics > Rendering Options), then set “HUD Plugin” to “Enhanced HUD” (Graphics) – the old-school interface and renderer are… products of their time. Interesting on a technical level but sort of nauseating to actually play with. Even I, a massive authenticity snob, prefer to play with these ameliorations.

4.7. Portal (2007), Portal 2 (2011)

I mean come on, it’s Portal. You’ve heard of these two. They haven’t had “Overwhelming Positive” ratings for 18 and 14 years as just some kind of joke. We’re talking about some staggering masterpieces here!

They are pretty different, though. Portal is short, understated, and a little horrifying; Portal 2 is overtly comedic and more character-driven. I can’t say which you might prefer.

For a long time I was personally more of a Portal 2 guy, but I’ve recently undergone a little reversal of favor. 2 is still great, of course, and I still have some quibbles with 1, but MAN. The atmosphere in the original is second to none.

I’ve seen people call it “sterile”, but I have to disagree. Portal 1’s Aperture is a place that used to be sterile. Ever see that news crew footage of the Mexia Supermarket cleanup?

The structures we build to live in, to work in, to shape and shelter ourselves and our communities, degrade shockingly quickly into lethality. Benzene, acetone, lead, asbestos, gas leaks, mycotoxins. Rust and rot alike consume to the point of hypoxia. It’s almost spiritual.
